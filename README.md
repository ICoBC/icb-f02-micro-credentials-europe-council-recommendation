# Micro-credentials in Europe: the Council Recommendation in Practice

*The European approach to micro-credentials and what it asks of issuers.*

## About this course

This course explains how the European Union describes micro-credentials and what that means for the people who design, issue, register and recognise them. It is course ICB-F02 in the ICoBC Kursbibliothek. It is aligned to the ICoBC Credentialing Skills Framework (ICSF), skill POLR "Policy and regulatory navigation" (VRITE pillar: Value), level L2 "Operate", stage K "Knowledge".

At this level the skill is: identify which rules apply to a credentialing activity, follow the related procedures and escalate questions. This course covers the Knowledge stage: you learn the concepts, rules and reasons, and you show that you can explain and recognise them.

**Who this course is for**

- Programme and micro-credential leads
- Quality, registry and admissions staff
- Professional bodies and sector associations
- Policy makers and public agencies
- L&D and corporate academy leads

1. Explain the EU definition of a micro-credential and the standard elements it should carry.
2. Apply the ten principles for design and issuance to an example and identify gaps.
3. Describe how micro-credentials relate to the EQF, national qualification frameworks and ECTS.
4. Identify what corporate academies must add if they want education providers to recognise their credentials.

## Policy context and definition

### Why Europe has a shared approach

Short learning experiences have grown across Europe. Universities, colleges, training providers, professional bodies, public agencies and companies all offer them. Without a shared approach, learners and employers struggle to compare them, and education providers struggle to decide whether to recognise them.

In 2022 the Council of the European Union adopted a Recommendation on a European approach to micro-credentials for lifelong learning and employability. A Council Recommendation is a policy instrument. It is not a regulation, and it does not create a legal duty for an issuer. It sets out a shared approach that Member States are invited to follow and apply through their own systems.

This matters for your daily work. When a colleague asks "does the EU require this?", the careful answer is that the Recommendation describes agreed good practice and expectations, and that national rules decide what is binding in your country. If you are unsure which national rules apply, escalate the question. That is part of the L2 skill in this course.

The Recommendation has three broad aims in practice:

- to help learners find and compare micro-credentials,
- to help employers and education providers understand what a micro-credential shows,
- to support trust between issuers across borders.

The Recommendation sits alongside other European tools. Two are central to this course: the European Qualifications Framework (EQF) and the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS). You meet both in module 4.

### The EU definition

The Recommendation defines a micro-credential as the record of the learning outcomes that a learner has acquired following a small volume of learning. Those learning outcomes have been assessed against transparent and clearly defined criteria.

Three ideas sit inside this definition:

1. **A record of learning outcomes.** The credential describes what the learner knows, understands and can do. It does not only say that the learner attended.
2. **A small volume of learning.** The learning is short compared with a full qualification. The definition does not fix a number of hours.
3. **Assessed against transparent criteria.** Someone assessed the learner, and the criteria were clear and published.

The Recommendation also says that learning leading to a micro-credential is designed to provide the learner with specific knowledge, skills and competences that respond to societal, personal, cultural or labour market needs. Micro-credentials are owned by the learner, can be shared, and are portable. They can be standalone or combined into larger credentials. They are backed up by quality assurance following agreed standards in the relevant sector or area of activity.

<!-- VERIFY: check the exact wording of the Council Recommendation definition and the surrounding text against the official Journal text before publishing. -->

Notice what the definition leaves out. It does not say that a micro-credential must be digital, though digital issuance is common. It does not fix a size, level or subject. It does not say who may issue. Module 5 returns to the question of non-formal and corporate issuers.

### Worked example and practice

**Worked example: a university short course and a sector association exam**

*University context.* A university's continuing education centre runs a four-week course on data protection for hospital administrators. The learner takes part in sessions, completes a case study and passes a written assessment marked against a published rubric. The centre issues a credential that lists the learning outcomes, the assessment method and the assessment criteria. This fits the definition: a record of learning outcomes, a small volume of learning, assessed against transparent criteria.

*Professional body context.* A national association of accountants offers a half-day workshop on a new reporting rule. People who attend receive a certificate of attendance. There is no assessment. Under the EU definition this is a record of attendance, not a micro-credential, because no learning outcomes were assessed against criteria. The association can decide to add an assessment and learning outcomes, and then the record can qualify.

The lesson: the label does not decide whether something is a micro-credential. The content of the record and the assessment behind it do.

**Practice activity: test three offers against the definition**

For each offer, write down whether it meets the three parts of the definition and what is missing if it does not. Then compare your notes with a colleague or with the facilitator notes.

1. A public employment agency issues a digital badge to anyone who logs in to an online module. No test is given.
2. A training provider runs a two-day course in welding safety, assesses practical skills against a published checklist and issues a record listing the outcomes.
3. A sector association gives a "membership year" badge to all members who pay their fee.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>
Run this as a 15-minute pair discussion. Ask pairs to name the missing element for offers 1 and 3 (assessment and learning outcomes). Offer 2 meets the definition if the checklist is published and the record lists outcomes. Prompt discussion about borderline cases, such as an assessment that is only a self-declaration. Keep the focus on the three parts of the definition, not on the issuer type.
</details>

## The standard elements

### What the standard elements are

The Recommendation lists standard elements that a micro-credential should carry. They make the credential readable and comparable. A learner, an employer or an admissions officer should be able to find the same kinds of information in any micro-credential, wherever it was issued.

The Recommendation separates mandatory elements from optional ones. The mandatory elements are:

1. Identification of the learner
2. Title of the micro-credential
3. Country or region of the issuer
4. Awarding body
5. Date of issuing
6. Learning outcomes
7. Notional workload needed to achieve the learning outcomes, in ECTS credits where possible
8. Level of the learning experience leading to the micro-credential, with reference to the EQF and, where applicable, the national qualifications framework (NQF)
9. Type of assessment
10. Form of participation in the learning activity
11. Type of quality assurance used to underpin the micro-credential

The Recommendation also lists optional elements, which can be added where relevant. Examples include prerequisites needed to enrol, supervision and identity verification during assessment, grading scheme, integration and stackability options, and further information. Treat the optional list as a menu for issuers who want to give more context. Check the full list in the official text.

<!-- VERIFY: confirm the exact list and order of mandatory and optional elements against the Council Recommendation (Annex) before publishing. -->

### Why these elements matter: Rigor and Trustworthiness

The ICoBC VRITE model describes the qualities of a strong credentialing programme. Two pillars map directly onto the standard elements.

**Rigor.** Learning outcomes, type of assessment, level, workload and form of participation show how demanding the learning was and how it was checked. A reader can judge the substance of the credential.

**Trustworthiness.** Awarding body, country or region, date, learner identification and type of quality assurance let a reader check who stands behind the credential and whether the record is authentic and current.

Seen this way, the checklist has two jobs. It makes the learning visible (Rigor) and it makes the issuer accountable (Trustworthiness).

### Digital carriers and AI-generated content

In Europe, two tools are often used to carry these elements in digital form. The European Learning Model (ELM) is a data model that describes learning opportunities, qualifications and credentials in a common structure. European Digital Credentials for Learning is the EU framework and infrastructure for issuing, holding and verifying digital learning credentials. Both are in use by institutions across Europe. The ELM served as the data model for education credentials in the DC4EU wallet pilot.

A note on AI. Some issuers now use AI tools to draft learning content, outcome statements or assessment items. This does not change what a micro-credential must carry. The standard elements stay the same. The issuer remains responsible for the accuracy of every element, including learning outcomes that an AI tool helped to draft, and for the quality assurance behind the credential. A human with the right authority should review any AI-assisted text before it appears on a credential.

### Worked example and practice

**Worked example: reading the elements on two credentials**

*Training provider context.* A vocational training provider issues a credential titled "Safe handling of hazardous substances". The record shows the learner's name, the provider's name and country, the date, five learning outcomes, a workload of 1.5 ECTS credits (illustrative), a reference to EQF level 3, a practical assessment observed by a trainer, classroom participation and a statement that the provider is externally audited. All mandatory elements appear. A reader can judge both the rigor and the trustworthiness.

*Professional body context.* A professional body issues "Advanced practice in ethics" to its members. The record shows the member's name, the title and the date. It does not list learning outcomes, workload, level, assessment type, form of participation or quality assurance. Several mandatory elements are missing, so a reader cannot judge what the holder learned or how it was checked. The body can fix this by adding the missing elements to the credential template.

**Practice activity: spot the missing elements**

Read this sample record and list every mandatory element that is missing.

> Title: Introduction to project planning. Issued to: A. Learner. Issued by: Northfield Training Centre. Date: 3 March 2026. Learning outcomes: plan a small project; write a work breakdown structure. Workload: 20 hours (illustrative).

Write the missing elements and one question you would ask the issuer about each. You will use the same method in the gap analysis at the end of the course.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>
Suggested answer for the practice activity: missing items are the country or region of the issuer (unless implied), the level (EQF, NQF), the type of assessment, the form of participation and the type of quality assurance. The workload is given in hours; ask whether it can also be expressed in ECTS credits. Allow 20 minutes. In a live session, ask pairs to rank the missing elements by how much they would affect an admissions decision.
</details>

## The ten principles

### Overview

The Council Recommendation sets out ten principles for the design and issuance of micro-credentials. Issuers can use them as a check on their own practice, and quality staff and admissions teams can use them to judge a credential they receive. As a Recommendation, they describe good practice that Member States are invited to support.

The ten principles are:

1. **Quality.** Micro-credentials are subject to internal and external quality assurance.
2. **Transparency.** Micro-credentials carry clear information on the standard elements.
3. **Relevance.** Micro-credentials are designed to meet identified learning needs.
4. **Valid assessment.** Learning outcomes are assessed against transparent criteria.
5. **Learning pathways.** Micro-credentials are designed to support flexible learning pathways, including recognition of prior learning.
6. **Recognition.** Micro-credentials are designed to be recognised and, where relevant, to be accumulated.
7. **Portability.** The learner owns the micro-credential and can store and share it.
8. **Learner-centred.** Micro-credentials are designed with the learner's needs in mind.
9. **Authentic.** Micro-credentials can be verified to confirm they are genuine.
10. **Information and guidance.** Information and guidance on micro-credentials is available to learners and employers.

<!-- VERIFY: confirm the wording and order of the ten principles against the Council Recommendation before publishing. The short names above are summaries; check the official text. -->

### Reading the principles in groups

Ten items are hard to hold in mind, so it helps to group them by the question each answers.

**Is it good?** Quality, valid assessment and relevance. These principles concern the substance of the learning and the check on it.

**Can I read and check it?** Transparency, authentic and information and guidance. These principles concern how clearly the credential is described and how a reader can confirm it.

**Does it work for the learner and the system?** Learning pathways, recognition, portability and learner-centred. These principles concern what the learner can do with the credential.

Many gaps in real credentials fall into these three questions. A credential with excellent content and no verification fails the second group. A verified credential with unclear outcomes fails the first.

### Applying the principles to find gaps

To apply the principles, take a credential and ask one question for each principle. Record the evidence you find, and mark a gap when there is none. Typical gaps include:

- No external quality assurance, only internal checks (principle 1).
- Learning outcomes that are vague, such as "understand data" (principles 2 and 4).
- Assessment criteria that exist but are not shared with the learner (principle 4).
- No statement on whether prior learning can count (principle 5).
- A paper certificate with no way to verify it (principle 9).
- No guidance for employers on what the credential shows (principle 10).

Be precise. "The principle is not met" is less useful than "the credential lists no assessment criteria, so a reader cannot judge the standard applied". A precise gap statement helps the issuer to fix it, and helps you to escalate the right question.

## Levels, credits and frameworks

### The EQF and national qualification frameworks

The European Qualifications Framework (EQF) is a common reference framework with eight levels. Each level is described in terms of learning outcomes: knowledge, skills, and responsibility and autonomy. Level 1 is the most basic and level 8 is the most advanced. The EQF helps people compare qualifications from different countries by relating them to a shared set of levels.

Most European countries have a national qualifications framework (NQF) that is referenced to the EQF. An NQF organises the qualifications of one country into levels. When a country references its NQF to the EQF, a qualification at a given national level is linked to an EQF level.

Micro-credentials do not form a separate qualification system. A micro-credential can carry a reference to an EQF level and, where applicable, an NQF level, as one of the standard elements. Whether a national system places micro-credentials in its NQF depends on national rules. This is a point where you should check the current position in your country and escalate if unclear.

<!-- VERIFY: check current national arrangements for placing micro-credentials in the NQF for the countries relevant to your audience before publishing. -->

A level on a micro-credential describes the learning experience. It does not turn the micro-credential into a full qualification at that level. A person with a level 6 micro-credential has not gained a level 6 qualification. Readers should interpret the level as a description of the demands of that learning experience.

### ECTS and workload

The European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS) expresses learning workload in credits. It is widely used in higher education. One ECTS credit represents a notional amount of student workload, and a full-time academic year is usually described as 60 credits. ECTS credits describe workload linked to learning outcomes. They do not describe quality or difficulty.

The Recommendation asks that the notional workload of a micro-credential be stated, in ECTS credits where possible. "Where possible" matters. A university course can usually express its workload in ECTS credits. A provider outside higher education may not use ECTS, and may state workload in hours. In that case the credential should still say clearly how much learning effort it represents.

Workload is notional. It is an estimate of the time a typical learner needs to reach the learning outcomes, and it includes study, practice and assessment, not only contact time.

An ECTS credit on a micro-credential supports recognition by an education provider, but it does not force recognition. Each provider decides on recognition under its own rules and the applicable national rules. A micro-credential that states 3 ECTS credits does not carry an automatic right to 3 credits in another programme.

### Stackability, ELM and what the numbers do not say

The Recommendation encourages micro-credentials that can be combined, or stacked, into larger credentials. Stackability is an option for the issuer and a design decision. It depends on how the micro-credential relates to a programme, and a statement on stackability helps learners and recognising bodies. The ELM can describe how a micro-credential relates to a larger qualification, and European Digital Credentials for Learning can carry that information in digital form.

Take care with three common errors:

1. Treating the EQF level of a micro-credential as a qualification level for the holder.
2. Treating ECTS credits as a promise of recognition.
3. Assuming every country places micro-credentials in its NQF in the same way.

When you meet a credential with an EQF level and ECTS credits, read them as information about the learning experience. Then check the recognition rules of the education provider or competent authority that matters for your decision.

## Corporate and non-formal issuers in the European model

### Who issues micro-credentials

The European approach is open to many types of issuer. Universities, colleges and training providers issue micro-credentials. So do professional bodies, sector associations, public agencies and companies, including corporate academies. The definition and the standard elements apply to all of them. Nothing in the definition limits issuing to formal education.

Openness does not mean automatic recognition. Education providers decide whether to recognise a credential for admission or credit. They work under their own rules, national rules and their quality assurance arrangements. A credential that carries all the standard elements is easier to assess, but it does not oblige a provider to recognise it.

For a corporate academy, the question is what the credential must show so that a provider can make an informed decision. The academy should treat that audience as a reader who does not know the company and who has to justify the decision to a quality office.

### What corporate academies must add

A corporate academy that wants education providers to recognise its credentials should be able to show the following.

1. **All the mandatory standard elements.** Clear learning outcomes, workload, level, type of assessment, form of participation and type of quality assurance. A reader outside the company cannot rely on internal job titles or internal course codes.
2. **Assessment that stands on its own.** Transparent criteria, and a clear statement on who assessed and how. Assessment by the same people who sold or ran the training may raise questions, so describe safeguards.
3. **Quality assurance a third party can understand.** Describe internal review and, where possible, external review, for example an independent audit or an external evaluation.
4. **A level reference with reasoning.** An EQF reference and, where applicable, an NQF reference, backed by a short note on how the learning outcomes were compared with the level descriptors.
5. **Verifiability.** A way for a reader to confirm that the credential is genuine and current.
6. **Plain language.** Outcomes in terms an academic reader can assess, not company jargon.
7. **A statement on use and recognition.** Say what the credential can be used for and what it does not claim. Do not suggest that it grants credit or entry.

None of this changes the learning itself. It changes how the learning is described and checked, so that an outside reader can judge it.

[(X)] The standard elements stay the same and the issuer remains responsible for them
[[?]] The tool used to draft text does not change what must be carried.
****
AI-generated learning content does not change the information a micro-credential must carry. The issuer remains responsible for accuracy and for the quality assurance behind the credential.
****

**Question 5.** A credential lists clear outcomes and criteria but is a plain PDF that cannot be verified. Which principle shows a gap?

[( )] Relevance
[( )] Learning pathways
[(X)] Authentic
[( )] Learner-centred
[[?]] Can a reader confirm the credential is genuine?
****
The authentic principle concerns verification. A plain PDF with no verification route leaves a gap even when the content is strong.
****

**Question 6.** Which gap statement is the most useful to an issuer?

[( )] The credential is weak on transparency
[( )] The assessment is not good enough
[(X)] The credential gives no assessment criteria, so a reader cannot judge the standard applied
[( )] The issuer needs a better reputation
[[?]] A useful statement says what is missing and why it matters.
****
A precise statement names what is missing and the effect on the reader. This helps the issuer to fix it.
****

**Question 7.** A professional body states that its micro-credential is at EQF level 5. What does this mean for the holder?

[( )] The holder has gained a level 5 qualification
[( )] Every education provider must recognise it at level 5
[(X)] The learning experience is described as corresponding to the demands of level 5
[( )] The national framework has been changed
[[?]] The level describes the learning experience.
****
The level is information about the learning experience. It does not create a qualification for the holder and does not force recognition.
****

**Question 8.** A micro-credential states 4 ECTS credits (illustrative). What is the correct reading?

[( )] Another programme must award 4 credits
[( )] The credential is of higher quality than one stated in hours
[(X)] The credential states a notional workload that can support a recognition decision by the receiving provider
[( )] The issuer is a higher education institution
[[?]] ECTS describes workload and does not decide recognition.
****
ECTS credits express notional workload. A receiving provider decides on recognition under its own rules.
****

**Question 9.** A corporate academy wants a college to recognise its credential. Which addition helps most?

[( )] A longer description of the company history
[( )] A promise that the college will gain company partnerships
[(X)] Plain-language outcomes, published assessment criteria, understandable quality assurance and a verification route
[( )] A change of the credential title to include the word "academic"
[[?]] The college needs information to judge the learning and the check.
****
An outside reader needs clear information on the learning, the assessment, the quality assurance and how to verify the credential. Marketing material and title changes do not provide this.
****

**Question 10.** A registry officer finds that an incoming micro-credential has all the elements but is unsure whether the issuer's quality assurance meets local expectations. What should the officer do?

[( )] Accept it to avoid delay
[( )] Reject it without further checks
[(X)] Follow the recognition procedure and escalate the question to the responsible quality office
[( )] Ask the learner to resit the assessment
[[?]] The skill at this level includes escalating questions.
****
At level L2 you identify which rules apply, follow the related procedures and escalate questions. The officer neither accepts nor rejects without checking.
****
